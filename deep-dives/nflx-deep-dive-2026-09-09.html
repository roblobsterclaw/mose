🎯 MOSE Verdict

Netflix has fallen roughly 39% from its 52-week high of $126.70 and sits close to its 52-week low of $65.08 — and the business underneath that fall grew revenue 13.4% year over year in Q2 2026, earned a 33.4% operating margin, and is on track for about $12.5 billion of free cash flow this year. The gap between the price action and the fundamentals is the whole idea.

The market is not pricing bankruptcy risk here. It is repricing a growth stock into a mature one: subscriber additions are slowing, Q3 revenue guidance came in light, and management said it will publish fewer engagement updates — which investors read, fairly, as a company getting quieter as the numbers get harder. That de-rating is real and may not be finished.

🏰 Business & Moat

Netflix is the world's largest subscription streaming service, with roughly 325 million paid memberships. The moat is not technology — anyone can stream video. It is scale economics in content: Netflix spends more on programming than anyone and amortises it across more subscribers than anyone, so its cost per subscriber-hour is structurally lower than every competitor. That advantage compounds, because the cheapest producer can outbid rivals for the next hit and still earn a better margin.

Three things have changed the earnings profile since 2022. Paid sharing converted password freeloaders into payers. Price increases stuck without meaningful churn, which is the clearest evidence of pricing power a subscription business can give you. And the advertising tier opened a second revenue stream on the same content base — ad dollars flow to the bottom line at very high incremental margin because the content is already paid for.

The ad business is the part of this story most worth watching. It is expected to roughly double to about $3 billion in 2026, and Netflix disclosed something genuinely new: ad-tier subscribers now show higher intent to stay than premium subscribers — the first time that has been true. Cheaper customers who churn less is the opposite of what the bear case assumes.

📈 Bull Case

  • Growth is still 13.4% with a 33.4% operating margin — this is not a broken business.
  • ~$12.5B free cash flow guided for 2026, against a market value near $326B: a ~3.8% FCF yield on a franchise growing double digits.
  • Advertising doubling to ~$3B, at high incremental margin on content already paid for.
  • Ad-tier retention now beats premium — undercuts the "cheap tier cannibalises" fear.
  • Demonstrated pricing power: repeated increases without material churn.
  • Live events and sports remain a large, mostly unpriced option.
  • Multiple has compressed from a growth premium toward the market — the de-rating has largely happened.

📉 Risks

  • Subscriber growth is decelerating, with roughly 3 million of full-year headwind attributed to the 2026 World Cup.
  • Q3 revenue guidance of $12.86B came in below the ~$13B the street expected.
  • Management is reducing engagement disclosure. Less transparency exactly when growth slows is a legitimate governance concern, and it is why the stock fell 7% on the print.
  • Operating margin slipped to 33.4% from 34.1% a year earlier.
  • Streaming is maturing — Disney, Amazon, YouTube and Apple all compete for the same hours, and content costs inflate with competition.
  • Barton and Coatue trimmed: the longest-standing holders are reducing.
  • MOSE has no published owner-earnings model for Netflix — the valuation figures here are estimates, not a DCF.

⚠️ Read the multiple as an estimate, not a fact. Share count is derived from reported EPS and net income rather than taken from the balance sheet, and Netflix completed a 10-for-1 split, so every historical price you see elsewhere needs adjusting before comparison. The direction is solid — roughly 24× earnings and a ~3.8% FCF yield for a business compounding low-to-mid teens with 31.5% margins — but do not treat the decimal places as precise.

The simple frame: you are paying a market multiple for a business with pricing power, a structural cost advantage, a second revenue stream just starting to scale, and $12.5 billion of cash coming in the door. The reason it is cheap is decelerating subscriber growth. The reason Ackman and Terry Smith bought is that revenue, margins and cash flow do not care how the subscriber number is framed.
